## "Additional local offices may appear"

Fair hit — I answered a question you didn't ask. "Local" means **municipal**: mayor, city council, school committee. Ballot questions aren't offices, and party committee seats are party offices, not local ones. Neither belongs in the answer.

The actual answer: [Malden elects its municipal offices in odd years](https://www.cityofmalden.org/DocumentCenter/View/9842/2025-Candidates-Handbook-PDF) — last Nov 2025, next Nov 2027. Local offices reach a state primary ballot only when a city stacks a municipal special on the same day. [Malden's 2026 special — the Prop 2½ override — was March 31](https://advocatenews.net/malden/news/maldens-proposition-2-1-2-override-proposal-gains-support-from-elected-officials-city-personnel/).

# Appendix: the tiebreak math

## Setup

N other voters. A gets $S \cdot N$, B gets $(1-S)N$. $S$ is a random variable — I don't know the true share, and that uncertainty, not sampling noise, is what the whole calculation turns on.

Define margin $M = 2S - 1 \in [-1, 1]$. Model $M \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$.

## Derivation

Vote difference $D = (\text{A votes}) - (\text{B votes}) = MN$. $D$ is an integer with the same parity as $N$, so achievable values are **spaced 2 apart**. An exact tie is $D = 0$.

Change of variables: since $D = MN$, the density of $D$ is $f_D(d) = f_M(d/N)/N$. At $d = 0$:

$$f_D(0) = \frac{f_M(0)}{N}$$

The probability mass on a single lattice point ≈ density × spacing ([local limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_limit_theorem) — the discrete distribution is locally flat at this resolution because $1/N \lll s$):

$$P(\text{tie}) \approx f_D(0) \cdot 2 = \frac{2 f_M(0)}{N} = \frac{2}{N} \cdot \frac{1}{s}\varphi\!\left(\frac{m}{s}\right)$$

where $\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$.

Sanity check on the shape: $P \propto 1/N$ — ten times the voters, one tenth the chance. And $P \propto \varphi(m/s)/s$ — it depends on how many *standard deviations of forecast error* separate you from a tie, not on the raw lead. This is the [Gelman–Silver–Edlin](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/probdecisive2.pdf) construction ([Econ. Inquiry 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1465-7295.2010.00272.x)), following [Gelman–King–Boscardin 1998](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/vote.pdf) and [Chamberlain–Rothschild 1981](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0022053181900187).

## Why your objection was half right

"An 82% race won't come within one vote" — correct that it's astronomically unlikely *given* a known 58–42 split. If $S$ were known exactly and votes were [iid Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_distribution), $P(\text{tie}) \approx \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi N \cdot 0.58 \cdot 0.42}}e^{-2N(0.08)^2/(0.58 \cdot 0.42)} \approx 10^{-3552}$.

But 82% is a *win* probability, not a vote share. It encodes $m/s \approx 0.92$ — i.e. Markey's expected margin is under one standard deviation of forecast error from zero. Binomial noise at N=800k has sd ≈ 0.0006 in share terms; forecast uncertainty is ~0.07. The second dwarfs the first by two orders of magnitude, so the binomial term is irrelevant and $10^{-5.4}$ is the right order.

Consistency check: my 82% implies $\Phi(m/s) = 0.82 \Rightarrow m/s = 0.915$. The table uses $.16/.14 = 1.14 \Rightarrow \Phi = 0.873$. Slightly inconsistent — I set $m,s$ from polling and the win probabilities separately with extra black-swan mass. Forcing consistency would raise the Senate figure ~7%. Immaterial at this precision.

## Where this is weakest

$s$ is the sensitive parameter and I guessed it: ~.14–.16 for polled races, .18–.20 for unpolled ones. Halving $s$ roughly doubles $P$ near a tie and collapses it in the tails.

The normal tail is doing all the work for MA-5 ($m/s = 4.6$) and GOP Gov ($m/s = 3.1$). Real forecast errors are [fat-tailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-tailed_distribution); a Student-t would give larger, more honest numbers. **Treat those two rows as lower bounds, possibly by orders of magnitude.** The three races with $m/s \approx 1$ are robust.

"Tie" ≠ "decisive." I compute $P(D=0)$. Being decisive also covers trailing by one and forcing a tie, so the decisiveness figure is within a factor of ~2 — [MA resolves primary ties](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVIII/Chapter53) by recount and party committee, not by your ballot. Factors of 2 don't matter when the spread across races is 10⁵.

$N$, $m$, $s$ and the win percentages are all my estimates.
